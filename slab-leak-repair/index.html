Warm Spot on the Floor, or a Water Bill That Jumped for No Reason?

A patch of floor that feels strangely warm underfoot, the sound of running water when every tap in the house is off, a sudden spike in your water bill, or water pressure that's dropped for no clear reason - on their own, each can mean something minor.

Together, or even one of them showing up clearly, this combination is the classic sign of a slab leak: a leak in a water line running beneath your home's concrete foundation. Because the pipe is buried under the slab, the leak stays hidden until it's caused real damage, which is why catching it early matters.

When it can't wait

When a Slab Leak Is an Emergency

A slab leak becomes an emergency once water reaches the surface. That means damp or warped flooring, water seeping through baseboards or up through a garage floor, a pool of water along the foundation outside, a floor that suddenly feels hot underfoot, or the sound of running water with everything off. Call us at any hour if you see any of these. Emergency slab leak detection is part of our 24/7 line.

While you wait, close the main water shutoff to stop the flow. If the leaking line is the hot side, turn the water heater off at the breaker or set the gas control to pilot so it does not run dry. Keep people off the wet floor and away from outlets in the affected area, and lift rugs and furniture legs off the damp spot.

A leak under the slab still needs proper detection before anyone breaks concrete, but stopping the supply first limits the damage.

Why it happens here

Slab Leaks Are a Real Risk in Lancaster

Lancaster's clay and sandy-loam soil expands when it's wet and contracts when it dries out, and that seasonal movement puts real stress on pipes running under a slab foundation - a genuine contributor to the joint failures and pinhole leaks that show up as slab leaks.

Add in decades of hard water accelerating corrosion on older copper or galvanized lines, and slab-foundation homes here are dealing with two real risk factors at once, not just one. We see this most often in the older east-side neighborhoods near Fox Field and in Downtown Lancaster's original housing stock, where original 1970s and 1980s supply lines are still common, though newer tracts out toward West Lancaster aren't immune once a line has had a couple of decades to corrode.

Detection

How Slab Leak Detection Works

  • 1

    Water meter check. With every fixture off, a moving meter dial confirms water is escaping somewhere in the system.

  • 2

    Acoustic and electronic detection. Listening equipment pinpoints the leak's location under the slab without guessing where to cut.

  • 3

    Targeted access. Once located, the repair opens only the section of slab or wall actually needed - not the whole floor.

Repair options

What Repair Actually Looks Like

Depending on the leak's location and the pipe's condition, repair options include a direct spot repair at the leak point, re-routing the line above the slab or through the attic to bypass the damaged section entirely, or trenchless pipe lining for a longer run of compromised pipe. Which option makes sense depends on the pipe's age and material - something we confirm during detection, not before.

Why it matters

What Happens If a Slab Leak Sits Unrepaired

A slab leak doesn't stay small. Water escaping under the foundation has nowhere to go but into the surrounding soil and, eventually, up through the slab into flooring and subflooring. In a home with tile or hardwood, that usually means warped or cracked flooring by the time the leak is visible without instruments - at that point, drying out the affected flooring becomes its own job alongside the pipe repair.

Left long enough, sustained moisture under a foundation can also affect the soil's ability to support the slab evenly, which is a much larger and more expensive problem than the plumbing repair itself. None of this happens overnight, but it also doesn't reverse itself - the honest math almost always favors an earlier repair over a later one.

Many Lancaster homes with slab foundations date to the city's growth years in the 1970s and 80s, which means copper and galvanized supply lines in the 40-to-50-year range are now old enough that a first slab leak is common, not unusual. If your home falls in that range and you've never had the plumbing under the slab inspected, a proactive check costs far less than waiting for symptoms.

A hot-water-side leak tends to show up first as a warm floor spot, while a cold-water-side leak is more likely to be caught only by the water meter test, since there's no temperature difference to feel underfoot - one more reason a meter check belongs in every inspection, not just a walk-through.

Where the slab is

Leaks Under Driveways, Garages, and Patios

Not every slab leak is under the living room. A supply or irrigation line can cross under a garage floor, a driveway, or a patio slab on its way to the yard. Water surfacing at the edge of a slab, along the foundation, or through a driveway crack points that way.

When you call, tell us where the water shows up and whether you think the leak is on the hot or cold side, and we will bring the right equipment for that part of the property.
