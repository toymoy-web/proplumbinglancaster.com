Why it happens here

Plumbing in the Antelope Valley Is Different

Lancaster sits in the Antelope Valley high desert, where summer highs regularly clear 95–100°F but a strong overnight cooldown still drops temperatures into the 60s - and colder in winter. That swing matters for plumbing: an exposed hose bib or an uninsulated line can still crack on a cold desert night even after a hot day. A frost-free spigot solves most of that risk, but only if any attached garden hose is disconnected before the cold sets in - a hose left on defeats the design and lets the stem freeze anyway.

The bigger factor is the water itself. Lancaster draws from Antelope Valley groundwater blended with imported California Aqueduct supply delivered through the State Water Project. That's a different system from the older Los Angeles Aqueduct, completed in 1913, which turned Lancaster into a boomtown while it was under construction nearby but was built to carry Owens Valley water to the city of Los Angeles, not to supply Lancaster itself. The mineral content in Lancaster's actual water supply runs extremely hard today. That scale buildup is what shortens water heater life and narrows galvanized pipe from the inside - especially in homes built during Lancaster's 1970s–80s growth years that still have original steel supply lines. The valley's clay and sandy-loam soil adds another wrinkle: it expands and contracts with the seasons, which is a real contributor to slab leaks and sewer line joint failures in older neighborhoods.

Common calls

What Lancaster Homeowners Call Us For Most

Leak-related calls make up the largest share of what comes in from this area - hidden slab leaks, water heater leaks, and leaks along the water service line between the meter and the house. Clogged and slow drains are the next most common, usually tied to the same hard-water scale that clogs a water heater, and sewer line calls tend to spike where mature trees sit close to older sewer laterals. Toilet repairs, faucet and valve issues, and garbage disposal problems round out the rest. None of this is unique to any one street in Lancaster - it's the pattern across a high-desert city with a mix of older slab-foundation homes and genuinely hard water.
