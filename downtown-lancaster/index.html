Where this is

What We Mean by Downtown Lancaster and The BLVD

Downtown Lancaster is centered on Lancaster Boulevard, known locally as The BLVD. Wikipedia's entry on the city describes The BLVD as a one-mile stretch of Lancaster Boulevard between 10th Street West and Sierra Highway, completed in 2010 and designated The BLVD Cultural District in 2018. Our page covers that corridor and the nearby blocks. The city does not publish a hard boundary for the area, so we describe it by its anchor streets and do not pretend to know where one neighborhood ends and the next begins. (source)

The downtown is also where the city began. The same entry notes that the Southern Pacific line through the Antelope Valley was completed in 1876 and that Moses Langley Wicks bought railroad property in 1884 and laid out the town. We mention that as history only. A building's location tells us nothing certain about the pipes inside it, so we look at the building in front of us.

What is there

A Mix of Storefronts, Restaurants, Civic Buildings and Housing

The City of Lancaster's Downtown Lancaster Specific Plan describes the goal as a destination with a mix of commercial, residential and transit uses, and it sets form-based zoning for the area. The Congress for the New Urbanism, writing about the 2010 streetscape rebuild, credits the effort with just over 800 housing units built or rehabilitated and more than 116,000 square feet of commercial space built or renovated. (city plan, CNU summary)

Nearby public spaces include Sgt. Steve Owen Memorial Park, formerly Lancaster City Park, at 43063 10th Street West, American Heroes Park at 701 West Kettering Avenue, and Jane Reynolds Park at 716 Oldfield Street, which has Webber Pool. (parks list) Listing sites describe the blocks around downtown as a mix of ranch-style houses, newer homes, apartments and condos. We have not verified building ages, and we do not assume them.

Restaurants and shops

Grease, Floor Drains and Commercial Kitchens

A dining and shopping district puts steady demand on drain lines. General plumbing knowledge applies here. Kitchen lines that carry grease can build up and back up, floor drains in kitchens and back-of-house areas tend to be the first place a blockage shows, and a line that backs up repeatedly in the same spot is usually telling you something about what is inside it. We clear the line first, then look inside it with a camera so you know whether it was a one-time clog or a recurring problem. See our drain cleaning and hydro jetting pages for how each approach works.

Rules for grease interceptors and for discharge into the sewer are set locally, so ask the City's building department and your sewer provider before a build-out or a fixture change. Many commercial properties also have backflow prevention assemblies that need periodic testing. Ask your water provider what applies to your property, and see our page on backflow testing and prevention.

Apartments and lofts

Shared Systems: Who Fixes What

In an apartment, condo or loft building, the first question is usually who owns the problem. A leak that starts in one unit and shows up in another, a water heater that serves several units, or a drain line shared by a stack of bathrooms all involve the owner or the property manager. If you are a tenant, report the problem to your manager in writing, take photos, and ask where the unit's shutoff valve is before something goes wrong.

If you manage the building, a good first step is to find and label every shutoff valve and to know which fixtures share a line. When a leak is hidden in a wall or ceiling, leak detection can find the source without opening more of the building than needed. For tank problems, our water heater repair page covers the signs that point to repair versus replacement.

Not sure who should be called? Tell us whether it is a home, a rental or a business and we will point you in the right direction.

Sewer lines

Who Owns the Line Between the Building and the Main

For the sewer systems it maintains, the LA County Public Works Sewer Maintenance page says the sewer lateral is owned entirely by the private property owner, for its whole length including the part under the street, and that the district maintains the public mains. (source) Ask the City of Lancaster which agency maintains the main on your street, and check your lease if you rent, because it may say who handles lateral repairs. In older commercial blocks, one lateral can serve more than one space, so it is worth confirming what is shared before you plan any work.

Before you call

Questions About Downtown Lancaster Plumbing

We use Downtown Lancaster to mean the Lancaster Boulevard corridor known as The BLVD and the blocks around it. The city does not publish a fixed boundary, so we ask for your address when you call and confirm coverage then.

Describe the property, the problem and how many drains are affected when you call. Businesses often need a camera look at a repeating backup, and we plan the visit around what you tell us.

Start with your property manager or landlord and put it in writing with photos. If water is actively spreading, close the shutoff valve for your unit if you know where it is, and ask your manager about the building's main shutoff.

For the systems LA County Public Works maintains, the County states the lateral belongs to the property owner and the public main belongs to the district. Ask the City which agency serves your street, and read your lease if you rent.
