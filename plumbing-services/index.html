What counts as an emergency

What We Treat as a Plumbing Emergency

Call at any hour for any of the following. A burst or cracked pipe, water coming through a ceiling or wall, a sewer line backing up into tubs, toilets, or floor drains, a water heater leaking from the tank, an overflowing toilet that will not stop, a refrigerator or washing machine line spraying water, a broken valve you cannot shut, or no water at the house at all.

Each of these gets worse with time, and each has a first step that you can take while we are on the way.

For a suspected gas leak, leave the building first and call 911 or your gas utility's emergency line from outside. Call us for the repair after the utility has made it safe.

No water

No Water at the House: Where to Start

When the water goes off, check whether neighbors are affected, since a street-side outage is a utility problem. If only your house is dry, look at the main shutoff valve to be sure it is fully open, and check whether pressure at the hose bib is gone or only reduced.

A pressure tank that has failed on a well system, a broken line between the meter and the house, a closed or damaged valve, and a frozen or burst pipe are all common causes. Tell us what you found when you call and we will start with the likeliest cause.

Leak repair

Leak Repair: Finding It First, Then Fixing It

A leak that you can see is only half the answer. Bathtub and sink leaks, a leak under the house, and a ceiling leak below a bathroom can all start somewhere other than where the water shows up. We find the source, repair it, and check the surrounding pipe and fittings so you are not calling again in a month.

For leaks that are hard to locate, our leak detection service brings acoustic and thermal equipment to find the exact spot.

Landlords, property managers, and tenants can all call us. If you are calling on behalf of someone else, have the address, the type of leak, and a contact who can give access.

Water pressure

Low Water Pressure: Pipe, Valve, or Street?

Weak pressure at every fixture often means a partly closed main valve, a failing pressure regulator, or aging galvanized pipe that has narrowed over the years. Low pressure at only one fixture is usually a clogged aerator, cartridge, or supply line at that fixture. We check pressure with a gauge before we recommend anything, so you know whether the fix is a valve, a regulator, a section of pipe, or something on the utility side.
