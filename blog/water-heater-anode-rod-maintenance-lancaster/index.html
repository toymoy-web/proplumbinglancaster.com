Water Heater Maintenance Checklist: The Part Almost Everyone Skips

Flushing gets all the attention. The anode rod is the part actually standing between your tank and internal rust failure.

Flushing sediment isn't the only water heater maintenance task that matters in Lancaster - there's a second component almost nobody checks, and it's the one actually standing between your tank and a slow, internal rust failure.

The part nobody thinks about

What a Sacrificial Anode Rod Actually Does

Nearly every tank water heater has a sacrificial anode rod - typically magnesium, aluminum, or a zinc alloy - hanging inside the tank specifically to corrode instead of the tank's steel shell doing so. Through a straightforward electrochemical process, the rod attracts the corrosive reaction in the water, sacrificing itself so the tank's steel shell doesn't rust through instead.

It's a genuinely simple piece of engineering, and it's also the single most overlooked maintenance item on a residential tank-style water heater. Tankless units don't use this same component, since they don't store standing water the way a tank does - this article is specifically about tank-style units.

Why Lancaster homeowners should care

What Happens Once It's Depleted

An anode rod typically lasts three to five years under normal everyday conditions, though water quality and household usage patterns shift that window in both directions depending on the specific property in question. Once it's fully consumed, the tank's own steel becomes the next sacrificial material in that same ongoing electrochemical process - meaning corrosion that was happening to a replaceable, inexpensive part starts happening to the tank shell itself instead.

By the time that shows up as rusty water or a visible leak at the tank's base, the unit is usually past the point of any reasonable repair.

The softener connection

If You've Installed a Water Softener, Read This

Softened water is more electrically conductive than hard water, and that higher conductivity speeds up the anode rod's electrochemical reaction - meaning a rod that would last three to five years in untreated hard water can deplete meaningfully faster once a softener is installed. This isn't a reason to avoid softening Lancaster's hard water. It's a reason to check the anode rod on a shorter interval afterward instead of assuming the original schedule still applies.

The full maintenance picture

Putting It Together With Flushing

Flushing addresses mineral scale settling on the tank bottom and heating element. The anode rod addresses a completely separate failure mode - internal corrosion of the tank shell itself. Doing one without the other leaves half the protection in place.

Doing both on a real schedule is what actually gets a Lancaster water heater closer to its full rated lifespan despite the local water conditions working against it. If it's been years since either was checked, that's worth bringing up on your next water heater service call.

What to look for

Signs an Anode Rod Is Already Depleted

  • ●

    A rotten-egg smell from the hot water, which can mean the rod's material is reacting with sulfur bacteria as it corrodes.

  • ●

    Rust-colored water appearing well after the tank has otherwise been flushed and cleared of loose sediment.

  • ●

    A metallic taste to hot water specifically, not present in cold water from the same house.

The inspection itself

What Actually Happens During a Check

Checking the rod means partially draining the tank, accessing a hex-head fitting on top of the unit (or, on some models, built into the hot water outlet), and pulling the rod to inspect how much of the original metal core remains versus how much has corroded away.

A rod that's visibly thin, coated in calcium buildup, or reduced mostly to its inner wire core is due for replacement regardless of how long it's technically been in service - the calendar is a guideline, but the rod's actual condition is what matters.

Choosing a replacement

Which Rod Material Actually Fits Your Situation

Magnesium offers the strongest galvanic protection and is the standard factory choice, but it reacts more readily with sulfur-reducing bacteria that some water supplies carry, which can produce a rotten-egg smell in hot water. Aluminum and zinc-alloy rods provide somewhat less aggressive protection but largely avoid that odor issue, making them a reasonable substitute for a household that's had that specific complaint with a magnesium rod installed previously.

There isn't a single correct answer for every home - it depends on which trade-off matters more for your specific water and your household's sensitivity to it. We can make that call together during a service visit rather than guessing at a replacement sight unseen.

The bigger picture

Why This Is Worth the Ten Extra Minutes

An anode rod costs a small fraction of what a full water heater replacement runs, and swapping one during a routine flush adds only a little time to a visit that's already happening. Skipping it doesn't save meaningful time or money in the moment - it just shifts a small, predictable cost later into a much larger, less predictable one whenever the tank itself finally gives out.

For a maintenance item this inexpensive relative to what it protects, there's very little practical argument for skipping it once a homeowner actually knows it exists and understands what it's quietly doing inside the tank.

Lancaster note

Check the Straps While You Check the Rod

California's Health and Safety Code section 19211 says new and replacement water heaters, and existing residential water heaters, must be braced, anchored, or strapped to resist falling or horizontal displacement from earthquake motion. It covers standard units up to 120 gallons that have a readily available strapping kit. When the heater is accessible for an anode rod inspection, look at the straps too.

Hardness varies across the district. The LA County Waterworks District 40 2024 water quality report lists total hardness between 30 and 320 ppm, so how fast a rod is consumed depends on your water and the heater.
