What a Video Camera Sewer Inspection Actually Shows

It's mentioned on nearly every sewer-related page on this site - here's what the process and the footage actually reveal.

A camera inspection gets mentioned on almost every page of this site about drains and sewer lines, but few homeowners actually know what the process looks like or what the footage reveals. Here's what actually happens during one, and why it's become the standard first step before any real sewer line diagnosis.

The equipment

What's Actually Going Down the Pipe

A sewer camera is a waterproof, high-resolution camera head mounted on a flexible fiberglass rod, fed into the line through an existing cleanout or another access point. The camera transmits a live video feed to a monitor the technician watches in real time, and a locating transmitter in the camera head lets us pinpoint the camera's exact position from above ground using a receiver - useful for marking precisely where a problem sits before any digging or lining work begins.

Reading the footage

What We're Actually Looking For

A trained eye is looking for specific, distinguishable patterns: a root mass growing through a joint looks visually different from a grease buildup coating the pipe wall, which looks different again from an offset joint where two pipe sections no longer line up cleanly, which looks different from a genuine crack or collapse. Water flow patterns during the inspection also matter - standing water pooling in one section (a "belly" in the line) points to a different problem than water flowing normally past an otherwise-damaged section. This is exactly what separates the right fix, whether that's hydro jetting, trenchless lining, or excavation, from a guess.

Beyond diagnosing a current problem

Real Estate and Preventive Uses

A camera inspection before buying a home with an unknown sewer line history is a genuinely useful, increasingly common step in a real estate transaction - it can reveal root intrusion, an aging clay line nearing the end of its service life, or a section already lined by a previous owner, none of which show up in a standard home inspection. For a current homeowner with an older Lancaster property and no known sewer line history, the same inspection works as a proactive check rather than waiting for a backup to force the question.

What you get afterward

Documentation You Can Actually Use

The inspection footage itself is real documentation - useful for a repair estimate, an insurance claim if damage is involved, or simply a record of the line's condition at a specific point in time that you can reference again years later. We can provide footage or a written summary of findings after an inspection, so you're not just taking our word for what the camera found.

How long it actually takes

What to Expect During the Visit

A standard residential inspection typically runs a reasonable portion of a service visit, not an all-day undertaking - the camera is fed through at a controlled pace, pausing at anything worth a closer look, then withdrawn once the full accessible length has been covered. Access matters more than length for how quickly it goes: a property with a well-placed, easily reached cleanout moves faster than one where we need to identify an alternate access point first.

What it can't tell you

The Honest Limits of a Camera Inspection

A camera shows the interior condition of the pipe clearly, but it can't measure exactly how much soil is missing around a section that's shifted, and it can't predict with certainty how many more years a visibly aging but still-intact line has left. It's a diagnostic tool for what's happening right now inside the pipe, not a crystal ball - which is exactly why an honest assessment sometimes includes a recommendation to re-inspect again in a year or two for a borderline case, rather than forcing an immediate repair decision on ambiguous footage.

Before and after a repair

Confirming the Fix Actually Worked

A follow-up camera inspection after a repair or a trenchless lining job is a genuinely useful step, confirming the fix looks the way it should from the inside rather than just assuming it worked because the immediate symptom went away. This is standard practice after any lining job specifically, since the liner's proper adhesion and smooth interior finish are best confirmed visually rather than assumed based on the fact that water is flowing normally again.

A reasonable expectation

How Often Should a Healthy Line Be Re-Checked?

There's no universal answer, but a property with older clay or cast iron pipe, mature trees near the line's path, or a known history of prior issues benefits from a periodic check-in - every few years, rather than only after a backup forces the question. A newer PVC line with no known problems and no nearby aggressive-rooted trees generally doesn't need the same proactive schedule. We can give you an honest, specific recommendation for your own property's actual risk profile rather than a generic one-size-fits-all interval that ignores what's really happening underneath your particular yard.
